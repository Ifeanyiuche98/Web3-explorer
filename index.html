A wallet is your identity on the blockchain

It's not an app and it's not an account at a company — it's a pair of keys. The address is like your email address: you share it freely so people can send you crypto. The private key is like your password: whoever has it controls everything at that address.

There's no "forgot password" on a blockchain. Lose your private key and the funds are gone forever. That's why the saying exists: not your keys, not your coins.

Gas is the fee that buys your transaction a seat

A blockchain can only fit a limited number of transactions in each block. When demand is high, transactions compete by offering a fee — measured in gwei (a tiny fraction of ETH). Higher fee → picked sooner.

Think of it like an auction for limited space. Set your fee too low and your transaction waits. Set it higher and it jumps the queue.

Try it: Set your gas fee, send a transaction into the mempool (the waiting room), then mine a block and see if you made the cut.

The Mempool — transactions waiting to be confirmed

The mempool above holds pending transactions from other users, each offering a fee. When a block is mined, it takes the highest-paying transactions first (block capacity: 5).

Blocks bundle history into an unbreakable chain

A block is a batch of transactions plus a hash — a unique fingerprint of everything inside it. Each block also stores the hash of the block before it. That's the "chain" in blockchain: every block is locked to its history.

Mining is the search for a hash that meets the network's difficulty target. Change even one character anywhere in the history, and every hash after it breaks — instantly exposing the tampering.

Try it: Mine a few blocks, then click any transaction inside an old block to tamper with it. Watch what happens to the chain. Then repair it.

DeFi replaces the middleman with a math pool

A liquidity pool holds two tokens (here: ETH and USDC) deposited by users. Anyone can trade against the pool instantly — no bank, no exchange operator, no waiting for a buyer.

The pool follows a simple rule called the constant product formula: x × y = k. Every trade changes the ratio of tokens, which changes the price. Big trades move the price a lot — that's price impact, and it's why tiny pools give terrible rates.
